The leak

What a missed call actually costs your clinic

Keegan Brady · Physiotherapist & founder of Avilo · 1 September 2026 · 6 min read
← All articles

A patient texted your clinic at 9:14pm last night to cancel tomorrow's 8am. Nobody saw it until 8:30 this morning. The slot is gone, the patient is annoyed, and the $130 that existed at 9:14pm doesn't exist any more.

Most clinic owners think this happens occasionally. Then they count. Here's how to actually count it for your clinic, and what the number usually looks like when you do.

The three leaks, and what each one costs

1. The calls that ring out

When reception is with a patient and the phone rings out, two very different things happen depending on who's calling. An existing patient will usually try again. A new patient almost never does. They found you on Google, you didn't pick up, and the next clinic in the search results did.

The maths is brutal because new patients aren't worth one appointment. An initial consult plus a typical course of treatment puts the value of a single new patient somewhere between $400 and $700 for most allied health clinics. Miss two of those calls a week and you're leaving roughly $50,000 a year on the table, quietly, without a single line item to show for it.

2. The messages that sit overnight

Texts don't ring out, they just wait. And while they wait, things decay. A reschedule request answered in a minute keeps the patient and usually keeps the slot. The same request answered the next morning often loses the slot, because the moment when the patient was looking at their calendar has passed.

Count it for one month: pull every inbound text, count how many were reschedule or cancellation requests, and count how many got a reply in under 30 minutes. The gap between those two numbers, multiplied by your average consult fee, is the size of this leak. Most owners who run this exercise are surprised. Some are horrified.

3. The no-shows everyone saw coming

The patient who never replied to their reminder was tomorrow's no-show. Everyone at the desk could feel it coming. Nobody had time to chase it. A silent patient is close to a coin flip; a confirmed one almost always shows. Every unconfirmed appointment that becomes an empty chair is a full consult fee, gone, plus a practitioner standing around on the clock.

Run the numbers for your own clinic

You don't need software to do this once. You need an hour and last month's records:

  • Missed calls: check your phone system's log for rang-out and after-hours calls. Assume a third were new patients. Multiply by your new patient value.
  • Slow replies: count reschedule requests that waited more than 30 minutes, and how many of those slots ended up empty. Multiply by your consult fee.
  • No-shows: count last month's no-shows that were unconfirmed the day before. Multiply by your consult fee.

Add the three numbers. For a typical two-to-four practitioner clinic this lands somewhere between $2,000 and $6,000 a month. Not because anyone is bad at their job, but because the phone rings while someone is standing at the desk, and the correct decision in the moment, forty times a day, is to help the person in front of you.

The leak isn't a people problem. It's a bandwidth problem. The work arrives faster than a front desk can absorb it, and whatever overflows is invisible until you count it.

What to do about it

Start with the counting, honestly. If your number is small, congratulations, close this tab. If it isn't, the fixes stack in order of impact: make your reminders two-way so silent no-shows become visible reschedules, give someone clear ownership of chasing the unconfirmed list the day before, and put a backstop behind the phone for the hours nobody can answer it.

That last part is why I built Avilo. It reads and answers patient texts the moment they arrive, day or night, books reschedules straight into Cliniko, and chases the quiet ones before they become empty chairs. But whether you use software or a better roster, count the leak first. You can't fix a number you've never seen.
